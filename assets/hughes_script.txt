In 1905, a baby boy was born in Houston, Texas. By eighteen, he was worth millions. By fifty, he hadn't left his hotel room in years. This is the story of Howard Hughes.

Hughes inherited a fortune from his father's drilling equipment company — but money was never the point. As a teenager, he moved to Hollywood and produced films that pushed boundaries. His 1932 epic Scarface made his star, Paul Muni, a legend. He dated the most famous women in the world. He was the ultimate playboy billionaire.

Then Hughes discovered his real passion: aviation. He built his own aircraft company from scratch. He set speed records that stood for decades. In 1947, he flew a plane he designed himself across the United States in nine hours — the famous Spruce Goose. It was the largest flying boat ever built.

But success came with a price. Hughes suffered from severe obsessive-compulsive disorder and crippling anxiety. He became terrified of germs, of people, of the outside world itself. He started avoiding the public. Then he stopped leaving his room.

In 1966, Hughes moved into the Desert Inn hotel in Las Vegas — and simply never left. For nearly thirteen years, he lived in darkness, surrounded by piles of newspapers, surrounded by staff who communicated with him through notes slid under the door.

The world was changing outside those walls. The Vietnam War raged. Nixon resigned. Hughes watched it all on television, growing more paranoid, more isolated, more untouchable. He was worth billions — and completely alone.

On April 5th, 1976, guards finally broke into his suite. Howard Hughes was found dead in his chair, emaciated, with an IV needle in his arm. He was sixty-five years old. The medical examiner listed his cause of death as kidney failure.

But the strangest part of the story? The government seized billions of dollars in assets — and couldn't agree on what to do with them. Hughes had left no will. His estate became one of the most complicated legal battles in American history.

Today, Howard Hughes is remembered as a cautionary tale — proof that genius and wealth mean nothing without peace of mind. The man who flew faster than anyone alive couldn't find a way to live. This is Howard Hughes.
