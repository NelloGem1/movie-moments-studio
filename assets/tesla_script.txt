He gave the world alternating current, wireless power, and the radio — yet died alone in a hotel room, nearly broke. His name is on cars, rockets, and a unit of magnetism. This is the life story of Nikola Tesla.

Nikola Tesla was born in 1856 in Smiljan, a village in what is now Croatia, to a Serbian Orthodox priest and a mother who invented household appliances in her head. Young Nikola had a photographic memory and a habit of visualizing inventions completely in his mind — a talent that would define his entire life.

As a child, he built waterwheels and electric motors from scraps. He could see images so vividly he sometimes couldn't tell them from reality. He studied engineering in Graz, but dropped out after gambling away his tuition money — a decision he'd spend the rest of his life regretting.

Tesla's first real job was as a draftsman in Budapest, where he had his famous epiphany walking in a park — the idea for the alternating current induction motor, complete and working, appeared fully formed in his imagination. He was 26 years old.

In 1884, Tesla sailed to America with four cents in his pocket and a letter of introduction to Thomas Edison. He told Edison about his AC motor idea. Edison hired him — and when Tesla delivered, Edison famously refused to pay, calling him a poet. Tesla quit after six months.

Tesla then founded his own company, but his investors stole it. He ended up digging ditches for a dollar a day — the greatest electrical mind alive, working as manual labor. It was the lowest point of his life, and the beginning of his legend.

In 1888, Tesla sold his AC motor patents to George Westinghouse for a royalty deal. The timing was perfect — Edison was waging a vicious public campaign against alternating current, electrocuting animals to scare people. Tesla's clean, efficient AC won the war of the currents.

The victory became real in 1895, when Tesla's alternating current system powered the very first hydroelectric plant at Niagara Falls. The electrical grid that still runs the modern world runs on his design.

But Tesla wanted more than money. He became obsessed with wireless power — the idea of transmitting electricity through the air to every home on Earth for free. In 1899, he built a laboratory in Colorado Springs and produced lightning bolts 130 feet long.

During those experiments, Tesla claimed he detected strange signals from space — possibly the first detection of radio waves from distant stars, decades before anyone else. Whether it was extraterrestrial or just new technology, nobody knows.

His biggest dream was Wardenclyffe Tower — a 187-foot wireless transmission tower on Long Island funded by J.P. Morgan. Tesla promised free worldwide wireless power and communication. Morgan invested, then pulled out when he realized Tesla wanted it free for everyone — not profitable.

Without funding, the tower was demolished in 1917. Tesla's patents expired, his companies failed, and his mental health deteriorated. He became reclusive, claiming he'd invented a death ray and that he was communicating with aliens.

In his final years, Tesla lived in New York hotel rooms, feeding pigeons and going deeper into isolation. When he died in January 1943 at age 86, he was so alone that the Hotel New Yorker had to pay for his funeral. The FBI seized his papers, fearing enemy powers could weaponize his research.

But Tesla's legacy is everywhere. The unit measuring magnetic fields is named after him. The electric car company carries his name. Every power outlet in your home runs his alternating current. He held around 300 patents.

Nikola Tesla was a man who saw the future so clearly that the present couldn't keep up with him. He gave humanity electricity itself — and died believing the world had forgotten him. It hadn't. This is Nikola Tesla.
